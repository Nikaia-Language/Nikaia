# ADR-172: A `for` May Iterate Something Whose Step Pauses

**Status:** Accepted · **Built:** yes — see §5
**Date:** September 20, 2026
**Target Version:** Nikaia 0.0.94 (D5 in 0.0.95 and 0.0.96)
**Related:** [ADR-105](adr-105.md) D1 (`Seq[T]`, and the words after it that say
what one step may do), [ADR-025](adr-025.md) D6 (`throws` on a step, the shape
this copies), [ADR-121](adr-121.md) D4 (a worker's reply as something that can
be awaited), [ADR-055](adr-055.md) §5 (binding `tokio` is rejected),
[ADR-038](adr-038.md) (the I/O layer this language already has),
[ADR-119](adr-119.md) D2 (the `no_std` target a dependency would have to fit),
[Part III 17.1](../30-nikaia-tooling.md) (a stream larger than memory is a `for`
over `io::lines()`)

## 1. Context

`for line in io::lines() { … }` lowered to this:

```rust
for line in io::lines().await {
    let line = line?;
    …
}
```

The **call** awaited, because `io::lines` is not `sync`. The **step** did not:
`Lines::next` was an `Iterator::next` over a `BufRead`, which blocks the thread
it is on for as long as the line takes. So a `for` over standard input held its
thread for the whole loop, and at `user_parallelism = yes` that is a thread the
pool could have had.

`docs/open-decisions.md` carried this as a question for the owner rather than as
work, because it is a question about what a `for` **is** in this language and
not about how to build one. Rust has no stable `Stream`, so a step that pauses
is `while let Some(x) = s.next().await` below, and deciding that a `for` may
mean that is a decision about the construct.

**The shape already existed.** [ADR-025](adr-025.md) D6 put a word on a *type*
that changes how the emitter writes a `for`'s step — `throws`, answering *can it
fail*. A second word on the same type, answering *does it pause*, is one more of
the same thing rather than a new idea. And a `for` that cannot pause makes
`user_parallelism = yes` a promise the standard library itself breaks.

## 2. Decisions

### D1 — A `Seq` says whether its step **pauses**, and a `for` over one gives its thread up.

The word is `pauses`, after the sequence and beside `sync` and `throws`:

```toml
signature = "() -> Seq[String] pauses throws"
```

A `for` over such a sequence is written as one that awaits each step. Nothing is
refused and nothing is reported: a `for` over a stream is an ordinary program,
and the whole of this is that it stops holding a thread it does not need.

**Three states and not two, and that is the decision rather than a detail.** The
absence of `sync` was already readable as *a step may pause*, and taking it as
the claim would have been cheaper by one word. It is wrong, and the two ways of
being wrong are not symmetric:

* A sequence whose step **does not** pause, awaited: the emitted `.next().await`
  does not compile, and what a reader meets is `rustc` about a file nobody
  wrote — [Part III C.1](../30-nikaia-tooling.md) exactly, and C.4's correct
  program refused.
* A sequence whose step **does** pause, not awaited: a thread is held that could
  have been given up. A cost, and one this language paid on every `for` over
  standard input until today.

So the claim is positive. `sync` is a promise (*it does not pause*), `pauses` is
a warning (*it does*), and neither is *nobody said* — which is what a `map`'s
step is, since it runs the lambda and what the lambda does is the caller's. A
ledger that writes both words has said two things about one step and is not read
as either.

The verdict travels with the type, so naming the stream first is the same loop:
`let stream = io::lines()` and `for line in stream` await exactly as the inline
form does.

### D2 — The awaiting form is written off the checker's answer, never off the shape.

Which sequences pause is a claim in the ledger about a **type**, and the emitter
has no types ([ADR-028](adr-028.md)). So the checker records the loops whose step
pauses by the byte the statement starts at and hands the set over — the same
arrangement `fallible_loops` has, for the same reason and stated in the same
place.

The two compose without either knowing about the other: a loop that both pauses
and can fail awaits its step and takes the `?` inside, which is one ledger word
apart from a loop that does neither.

### D3 — The trait, when there is one, is `std`'s own.

There is **no trait today**, and that is the honest scope rather than a gap. Of
`Seq[T]`'s six producers in `std.contracts` — `io::lines`, `HashMap::keys`,
`HashMap::values`, `String::chars`, `Vec::drain`, `HashMap::drain` — five are
`sync` and exactly one can pause. What the emitted `while let` wants is an
inherent `async fn next` on one concrete type; a trait over many is owed the day
there is a second pausing producer, and is one trait rather than a language
feature.

**And it is not a foreign one**, which was asked and is answered here so that it
is not asked again:

* **`tokio` the runtime is already rejected**, by [ADR-055](adr-055.md) §5:
  *"It brings an executor and an I/O layer, and [ADR-038](adr-038.md) built the
  second one already. Two event loops in one process is the state D7 treats as a
  hazard."* Nothing in this question reopens that.
* **`tokio_stream::Stream` is not tokio's own trait** — it is a re-export of
  `futures_core::Stream` — so the dependency actually under discussion is
  `futures-core`: a trait definition, `no_std`-friendly, no executor. A far
  smaller thing than the runtime, and still the wrong place for it.
* **Where the cost really lands** is `Seq[T]`'s consumers — `collect`, `count`,
  `nth`, `join`, `map`, `filter` — which are `Iterator`'s below and need pausing
  twins. That work is the same whichever trait sits on top, and `futures-core`
  does not shorten it; `futures::StreamExt` would, at the price of `Pin`/`Poll`
  in our signatures and an [ADR-119](adr-119.md) D2 `no_std` target we no longer
  control.

So the trait is ours when it arrives, which is the move `Joined` made in
[ADR-170](adr-170.md). `futures_core::Stream` earns its place only **at a foreign
boundary**, where a described crate hands us a stream and its trait is the lingua
franca — never in the core.

### D4 — `io::lines` reads a chunk at a time, and the line endings are found here.

The first producer, and the one D1 was asked for. A step of it is a read of
standard input on an I/O worker, awaited, which is
[ADR-121](adr-121.md) D4's shape.

**A chunk and not a line**, and this is the difference between a change that pays
for itself and one that does not: a hop to a worker and back costs a wake-up, and
paying one per *line* would make `io::lines` slower than the blocking reader it
replaces. So `Lines` asks for a buffer, hands out the lines in it, and asks again
when it runs out — one hop per buffer.

**One read per hop and not a loop to fill the buffer.** Short is not the end of a
stream, and a caller that asked for a chunk wants whatever is there now: a pipe
that produces a line a second is a program that prints a line a second, and
filling 8 KiB first would make it a program that prints nothing for two hours.

What is handed out is dropped from the buffer before the next chunk goes in, so
what is held is the size of the *unread* remainder and not of everything the
stream ever carried. [Part III 17.1](../30-nikaia-tooling.md)'s *a stream larger
than memory is a `for` over this* stays true, which is the property this could
most easily have lost.

`BufRead::lines`' own rules are kept, because programs were written against them:
the newline is not part of the line, a `\r` before it is not either, a blank line
is a line, and a final line without a newline is still a line — but only once the
stream is over, or a chunk boundary would split a line at whatever byte it landed
on.

### D5 — An **eager** walk of a pausing sequence pauses and can fail; a **lazy** one is refused.

`Seq[T]`'s six consumers split on what they hand back, and the split is the
rule rather than a list of names:

* **Eager** — `collect`, `count`, `nth`, `join` hand back a *value*, so each is
  a loop around the step. Both of the receiver's words reach them: the walk
  pauses (D1) and, where a step can fail, the walk can fail. `std` writes them
  as inherent `async fn`s on `Lines`.
* **Lazy** — `map` and `filter` hand back another *sequence*, whose steps would
  pause. That is the trait D3 defers, so the call is refused from the lowering
  with the loop as the way out:

```text
error: `map` walks a sequence whose step pauses, and a `for` is the only walk
       of one this compiler can write yet (ADR-172 D5). Write the loop -
       `for line in io::lines() { … }` - and do inside it what this was going to
       do afterwards
  --> app.nika:4:5
   4 |     let ls = io::lines().map fn { a }
           ^
```

**A refusal from the lowering and no number**, which is
[ADR-171](adr-171.md) §4's rule: a number is a promise the rule stays, and this
is *this compiler cannot build that yet* rather than *the language forbids it*.
The day the lazy ones exist, the sentence goes and nothing in the catalogue has
to be withdrawn.

**The failing half is `NK2701`, the same code the `for` gets.**
[ADR-025](adr-025.md) D1 says a step that can fail fails the enclosing function
and that nothing marks the call; a `count` is a written call, so the rule
already covered it and only the compiler did not. The message says *a step of
what `count` walks* rather than *a turn of this loop*, because that is what a
reader is looking at.

**And that half's absence was a wrong answer, not a missing feature.**
`io::lines().count()` **compiled** before this, because `Lines` was an
`Iterator` over `Result<String, …>` — so it counted the *failures* as lines. The
ledger said `-> i64` and the program got one; nothing anywhere said the number
could be wrong. That is the bug class [Part I 6.4](../10-nikaia-light.md)
refuses by name, and it was in `std`. `collect` was the same shape: a list of
results, typed as a list of strings.

**One thing the emitter had to stop guessing.** `collect` is written
`.collect::<Vec<_>>()` below, because Rust's needs to be told what to build and
this emitter has no types. `Lines::collect` takes no type at all, so the
turbofish is skipped exactly where the **site pauses** — an `Iterator::collect`
never does, which makes that the difference rather than the method's name.

## 3. Consequences

A `for` over standard input gives its thread up. At `user_parallelism = yes`
that is a thread the pool gets back, and at `no` it is the same program it was.

**What it costs:** one word in the ledger's type language, one set threaded from
the checker to the emitter, and one buffer per stream. `Lines` stops being an
`Iterator`, which nothing outside `std` could see — the ledger names the type and
a program writes `for`.

**What it does not change:** every other loop. A container, a range, and a
sequence that says nothing keep the shape they had, which is asserted rather than
hoped for.

## 4. What this does not decide

* **The trait.** D3 says whose it will be, not what it looks like. That is
  written the day a second producer pauses.
* **The lazy consumers.** `map` and `filter` over a pausing sequence hand back
  one whose steps pause, which is the trait D3 defers. D5 refuses them meanwhile
  and `docs/open-work.md` carries it.
* **A `for` over a channel**, which is the other shape a pausing step could have
  and which nothing asks for yet.

## 5. What is built

All of D1, D2 and D4, and D3's answer is that there is nothing to build yet.

* `Ty::Seq` carries `pauses`; the word parses, writes itself back, and is refused
  beside `sync`. `fits` reads it the way it reads `throws`.
* `std.contracts` says `() -> Seq[String] pauses throws` on `io::lines`.
* The checker fills `pausing_loops`; the emitter writes
  `let mut __nikaia_sequence = …;` and `while let Some(x) = __nikaia_sequence.next().await`.
* `Lines` holds a buffer and has an inherent `async fn next`; the runtime has
  `Op::StdinChunk`.
* D5 both ways: `Lines` has `collect`, `count`, `nth` and `join` as inherent
  `async fn`s, a walk of a failing sequence raises `NK2701`, and the lazy ones
  are refused from the lowering. `io::lines().count()`'s silent miscount ended
  with the first of those, and the test that says so **runs the program**:
  three lines in, three out.
* `crates/nikaia/tests/pausing_steps.rs` is the word and the lowering, the line
  endings are `nikaia-std`'s own unit tests, and **`examples/tally/src/main.nika` is the
  proof that it runs**: five lines, two blank, longest 31 characters, through a
  pipe, end to end.
