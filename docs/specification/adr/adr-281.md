## 1. Question

**How does a program share a value that changes — what is the type called,
what is it in the machine, how is it read and changed, and what may run while
it is open?**

The pieces were decided one at a time, and each answer narrowed or completed an
earlier one: the type was named before it existed, its doors were given before
their blocks had a spelling, the shape underneath moved from a per-setting rule
to a per-value one, and the refusal of the lost update moved from one line of
syntax to a type. Read in order, eleven records describe a lock that no longer
exists at several steps. This record states the answer as it stands.

Without it, Part II 12.2's counter — the program `user_parallelism = yes`
exists to serve — is either unwritable or written against a rule a later record
had already changed.

## 2. How others do it

| Language / tool | Its answer | What it costs there | Source |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Rust** | two wrappers, `Arc<Mutex<T>>`; `RefCell` panics on a re-entrant borrow, a `Mutex` hangs on one, and a panic under a `Mutex` poisons it | the composition everybody complains about; two behaviours for what a reader thinks is one thing | [ADR-281](adr-281.md), this record's D4 |
| **Clojure** `atom`, **Haskell** `MVar` / `TVar`, **Scala** `Ref` | one name for the capability, no composition; the cell is made by a **call**, and a change takes a pure function | none of them can spell it as *a count around a lock*; the pure change is what allows compare-and-swap with retry | [ADR-281](adr-281.md)'s survey, not re-checked here |
| **C++** `std::lock` | several locks at once, by try-and-back-off | a different mechanism from sorting two by address | [ADR-281](adr-281.md) |
| **Perl** taint mode | data from outside is marked; one word removes the mark | somebody removes it early to make the types fit, and three functions later nobody remembers | [ADR-281](adr-281.md) |

What does not transfer: Rust's split into two wrappers, because the combination
is this language's common case; a mark that can be removed. What does: one name
made by a call, and a change written as a block the implementation may run more
than once.

## 4. Decision

### The type

### D1 — Shared mutable state is `SharedMut[T]`: one name for the capability, and it survives to the emitter.

`SharedMut[T]` means *a value several parts own at once and any of them may
change*. It does not mean *a count around a lock*; what is under it is the
compiler's, chosen per value (D5-D8). The short name is for the common case and
is a deliberate departure from spelling words out.

It is one name up to the emitter and becomes two hulls only there, so no
internal spelling appears in a diagnostic, a printed type or a ledger entry.
Naming the capability rather than the mechanism keeps the lock-free
implementation possible — which D8 uses.

### D2 — A hull you can observe, you write: `Shared(x)`, `SharedMut(x)` and `Locked(x)` are calls.

> A hull you cannot observe, the compiler writes. A hull you can observe, you
> write.

A `T?`'s `Some(…)` stays the compiler's ([ADR-278](adr-278.md)); these three
change when a value is cleaned up, which a program can see. A call is an
expression, so a hull is made wherever an expression stands — a literal, a
`return`, an argument, a field — and `SharedMut(0)` gives the number its type as
any argument does. The call is typed by the compiler, not the ledger: a ledger
signature binds from a receiver ([ADR-288](adr-288.md)) and a constructor has
none.

### D3 — `Shared[Locked[T]]` is refused, and `Locked[T]` is the lock on its own, for individually locked fields.

`NK1123` refuses the long spelling, and a hull of a hull, with the replacement
in the message. `Locked[T]` keeps its own use — one lock per field inside a
shared structure rather than one around the whole — and is opened by the same
doors. Part I 6.2 defines it by that use, not by the nesting.

### What is under it

### D4 — Two shapes stay, and the difference between them is made unobservable rather than bought away.

A cheap shape (a borrow flag) and a robust one (an operating-system mutex) both
stay. What a program could see between them — re-entering one lock panics under
one and hangs under the other — is made impossible at compile time (D28), not
paid for with a floor on every acquisition in every program. Poisoning is not
unified: at `no` a panic is a process abort (Part III A.2), so nobody survives
for whom poisoning would be done.

### D5 — The safe shape is the floor, and the cheap one is an optimisation on top of it.

Where it is not **proved** that nothing crosses a thread with a value, its lock
is the mutex. [ADR-037](adr-037.md) D6's polarity, for its reason: wrong
towards the mutex costs nanoseconds, wrong towards the flag costs correctness.

### D6 — At `user_parallelism = no` every lock is the cheap shape.

Nothing a user writes can cross a thread there: one user thread
([ADR-037](adr-037.md) D2), and the runtime's I/O thread carries no closure and
cannot reach a user lock. The mutex would buy nothing and cost what the switch
exists to save — and the cheap shape says so when a lock is re-entered, where
the mutex hangs.

### D7 — At `user_parallelism = yes` the shape is decided per value, by the analysis that decides the count.

A lock is reachable from two places only through a shared handle, so the answer
`contracts::sharing` gives the handle is the answer for the lock inside it —
one analysis, two decisions, and every one of its reasons to decline carries
over unchanged.

### D8 — A word-sized value that crosses is the word, and its `update` is a compare-and-swap.

A third answer of the same analysis, beside `plain` (the cheap lock) and
`atomic` (the mutex): **`word`**, an atomic count around an atomic 64-bit word
whose `update` is D13's retry loop and whose `set(…; after:)` is one
compare-and-swap. A class of handles gets it where all of these hold:

* the count is `atomic` because of a **proven** crossing — a `spawn` body, a
  parallel walk — and not because the floor answered for something unseen;
* every handle is a `SharedMut` of a number, a `bool` or a `char`;
* no handle is named by an `access_all` or `update_all` (D18).

It never replaces the cheap shape, and at `no` nothing gets it.

### D9 — The shapes behave alike, and the mutex shape pays for it with an owner check.

`Rc` and `Arc` differ in cost and nothing else, which is what makes a per-value
choice invisible. A borrow flag and a mutex do not, so the mutex shape carries
an owner check and re-entering a lock says the same thing whichever shape a
value got. One behaviour, two prices. Whether that check is emitted at all is
D36's switch.

### The doors

### D10 — Four doors, and while the lock is open in `get` or `set` no user code runs.

| door | for | the block may wait |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| `kasse.get()` | taking a copy out | — |
| `kasse.set(value)` | replacing; the value is computed outside | yes, outside |
| `kasse.update fn(mut v) { v += 100 }` | changing it, under the lock | no |
| `kasse.access fn(state) { … }` | reading it in place | no |

`set` needs no block: its argument is evaluated before the call, so the lock is
open for one store, and `get` is one load. **While the lock is open in `get`
and `set`, no user code runs at all** — the sentence that replaces Part II
12.2's derivation through the `sync` lambda and [ADR-297](adr-297.md) D3's
*"for free"*, since `set` has no lambda.

**`get` and `access` are reads; `set` and `update` are writes.** Under
[ADR-292](adr-292.md) D3 two reads of one resource are unordered, so two `get`s
or two `access`es on one lock may run in either order, and the writing forms
keep theirs.

### D11 — `access` reads in place, and may not change what it is handed.

It hands the block the value where it lies and returns what the block computed.
It stays because nothing else reads part of a large value without copying it:
`get` copies, and asking a ten-thousand-entry list for its length should not.

### D12 — `update` has one form: the block takes `mut v`, changes it, and returns nothing.

```nika
kasse.update fn(mut v) { v += 100 }
protokoll.update fn(mut log) { log.push(entry) }
```

`mut` is [ADR-094](adr-094.md) D3's word, unchanged: the value is changed in
place, and the caller's value — here the lock's — is what changes. A block that
hands a value back is `NK1141`; one that changes `v` without the word is
`NK1138`, asked at a door. So exactly one lambda in this language is handed
something it may change, and it carries the word every changed parameter
carries; Part I 5.3's one lambda form is untouched.

### D13 — What `v` is, is the compiler's, by type: a copy where the value is a word, the address otherwise.

| the value | `v` is | the door does |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| in D8's `word` class | a **copy** on the stack | load the word, run the block on the copy, compare-and-swap it in; on a collision load again and run again |
| anything else | the **address** in the lock | take the lock, run the block once, release |

Nothing is moved out of the lock in either row and no slot is ever empty. The
program writes one form and learns nothing about word sizes or
`user_parallelism` from it.

### D14 — An `update` block may run more than once, and a program cannot tell.

The three things that would let a program notice are already refused at every
door: the block is `sync`, so it does not wait; it touches no lock, so it does
no I/O and takes nothing else ([ADR-288](adr-288.md) D21); and it captures its
surroundings as views (Part I 5.3), so it changes nothing outside `v`. The retry
is used only where the copy is free; a block on an address runs once, because a
change to the address cannot be taken back.

### D15 — A panic in the block leaves a word untouched and poisons an address.

On a copy nothing was swapped in. On the address the value stays as the block
left it, and D4 applies: an abort at `no`, poisoning at `yes`, where the next
door on that lock refuses.

### D16 — `get` is the one door that copies, and `--sharing` says where it copies something large and which row each value got.

`--sharing` names a `get` on a type that does not copy cheaply — *`big.get()`
copies the whole `String` out of the lock each time; `access` reads it in
place* — reports a word as `word` (*its `update` is a compare-and-swap and
there is no lock*), and says why a word-sized value that crosses kept its lock:
a door over several locks, or the floor.

### D17 — A door over several locks is a call the compiler types.

Not a ledger entry: a signature binds from a receiver and the locks are
arguments of different types. Not a construct: the grammar already takes
`access_all(a, b) fn(x, y) { … }` as a call with a trailing lambda, and a
keyword would cost a reserved word for nothing. Not a method on one lock:
`a.update_with(b)` reads as *first A, then B*, which is the bug. The checker
types the locks and hands the block one parameter per lock; the ledger carries
the `sync` column, `sync = "from(f)"`, from an entry with no signature.

### D18 — `access_all` reads; `update_all` takes one `mut` per lock, returns nothing, and keeps the lock.

```nika
update_all(konto_a, konto_b) fn(mut von, mut nach) {
    von -= 30
    nach += 30
}
```

D12 widened, not a second rule: nothing sees either lock between the two writes.
Two words cannot be swapped by one compare-and-swap on every target, so the pair
takes the locks, in D19's order — and a value such a door names keeps the
mutex rather than becoming a `word`, because a retry cannot be held. The word
shape is not a door over several locks, so a program that reached one with it
would not compile rather than race. Two locks at a time; `NK1124` refuses a
door handed something that is not a lock, one lock where two are wanted, or a
block that does not name one value per lock.

### D19 — The order is by address, decided at run time, and the names are bare.

`std` sorts the locks by address, so `access_all(a, b)` in one task and
`access_all(b, a)` in another take them alike, and no cycle can form. Two locks
need not hold the same type or have got the same shape. The names stand beside
`println`, without a `use` — [ADR-162](adr-162.md) D2 gives the reason.

### What leaves a lock

### D20 — Assigning to a `SharedMut` directly is `NK2204`, and the message names the door with the value in it.

`kasse = 42` is refused with *write `kasse.set(42)`*: the repair is mechanical,
and *"use a door"* would leave the reader to pick one of four. It fires where
the target is a **name**.

### D21 — What cannot be quoted back is not quoted.

An expression has no span ([ADR-081](adr-081.md) D2), so a help line rebuilds a
literal, a name and a negation and writes an ellipsis for anything else. A help
line that quoted the wrong thing would be worse than one that quotes nothing.

### D22 — What a lock hands out is `Seen[T]`, and the emitter erases it.

`kasse.get()` is a `Seen[i64]`; so is what `access` and `access_all` compute.
`Seen[T]` is a type in the checker and in the ledger's type language (Part III
13.5), and not in the language below: no counter, no marker, no check at run
time, no bytes.

### D23 — The stamp sticks, and passes through whatever cannot write it back.

`stand + 100`, `stand > 100`, `stand.abs()` are stamped. A call whose argument
is stamped is allowed where the callee's `touches` names no lock — it cannot put
the value into one — and its result is stamped: `f"…"`, `println`, `fs::write`,
a response body. A callee whose `touches` names a lock, or is absent (*touches
everything*, [ADR-292](adr-292.md)), takes a `Seen` only where its signature
says `Seen`.

### D24 — `Seen` is written at two places and inferred everywhere else.

A **struct field** that keeps a value read from a lock, because that is where
the value outlives the moment it was read; and a **parameter of a function that
touches a lock**, where a stamped value is passed in. Locals, expressions,
arguments to lock-free functions and results are inferred.

### D25 — A stamped value does not go back into a lock blind: three refusals, and no way around them.

* **`NK2205`, first shape:** a `set` given a stamped value —
  `kasse.set(stand + 100)`, whether `stand` was read on the line above, in
  another function or in another request. `kasse.set(kasse.get() + 100)` is
  its smallest case.
* **`NK2205`, second shape:** a `set` under a stamped condition —
  `if stand > 100 { kasse.set(0) }`. Conditions accumulate: a plain `if`
  inside a stamped one is still under it.
* **`NK2207`:** an `update` or `update_all` block that assigns to its `mut v`
  without reading it — a `set` through the back door. `v = v + 1` reads it.

There is no `.value`, no `overwrite`, and no word that turns a `Seen[T]` into a
`T`. `b.set(a.get())` is refused too; the form is
`update_all(a, b) fn(mut x, mut y) { y = x }`, which also reads a consistent
pair.

### D26 — `set(neu; after: seen)` is the one door for a stamped value, and it is a definition.

```nika
kasse.set(neu; after: stand)
// is, by definition,
kasse.update fn(mut v) { if v == stand { v = neu } else { throw Overtaken } }
```

The witness covers the stored value and the condition alike, so neither
`NK2205` shape applies. `T` must compare; the witness is a view; `Overtaken` is
an error like any other and carries nothing, because reading the current value
would be a second acquisition. It takes the lock once; on a `word` it is one
compare-and-swap, and on a large value it compares the whole value, which the
message says. The lowering's own name, `set_after`, written by hand is
`NK2208`: there is one door to the comparison, and so none around the stamp.

### D27 — The stamp's verdict is the same at `yes` and at `no`.

A two-line update at `no` with no pause between the lines would be safe, and is
refused all the same: the refusal is about the shape, not the clock
([ADR-005](adr-005.md) Group B).

### What may run while a lock is open

### D28 — Taking a lock while a lock is held is refused, always.

Part II 12.3's *"often a compile-time error"* becomes *always* (`NK2203`): one
door opened while another is open, directly, through a chain of calls, or by a
scope opened inside the block. With that no program exists in which the two
shapes of D4 behave differently. It displaces Part II 12.2's *"safety net"*
sentence and [ADR-005](adr-005.md) D6's last sentence, both of which said the
case was not refused statically.

### D29 — "Touches a lock" is a second derived property of a function, propagated over `sync`'s call graph with the opposite lattice.

One traversal, two lattices: `sync` is a greatest fixpoint (everyone starts
`sync`), this is a least one (nobody starts touching a lock). An unresolvable
call takes the `sync` claim away and sets this property, so fail-closed lands
on the same side for both. A trailing lambda's body and a **scope**'s tasks
belong to the calling function, because they run during the call; a `spawn`'s
body does not. No `from(f)` form is needed: the check reads the caller's own
body, where an immediate lambda is visible. Inside `update`, `access`,
`access_all` and `update_all`, a call to anything that carries the property is
`NK2203`. The ledger's column is `locks`.

### D30 — The property is coarse, and never answers an ordering question.

It says *a lock*, not *which*. The precise version needs alias analysis, and
then whether a program compiles would depend on whether two handles are
provably distinct. It is a different column from
[ADR-292](adr-292.md) D3's `touches`, which names a lock per container and is
what D10's ordering rests on.

### D31 — For this column, an absent entry means "touches a lock".

The one inverted key: an absent `sync` means *not `sync`*, and for `locks` the
restrictive answer is the other way round. Reading absence as permission is
the polarity [ADR-010](adr-010.md) D1 forbids. Part III 13.5 states it as a
sentence of its own.

### D32 — A call into foreign code is judged by what its arguments can reach, by the crossing walk generalised, never copied.

This language has no global mutable data, so foreign code touches only what it
reaches. Where no lock is reachable from the arguments, transitively through
fields, the call is allowed; otherwise it is `NK2503`, naming the call, the
argument and the field. The walk is `contracts::send`'s, because it already
answers *undecided* for an empty field list (`fs::Mapped`), carries a depth
limit and a cycle rule — judgements a copy would re-derive wrongly. It extends
[ADR-038](adr-038.md) D7's first rule to locks.

### D33 — A function handed to foreign code may not touch a lock, and the answer comes from its body, never its type.

It closes the callback detour: foreign code, called from inside an open lock,
calling back into Nikaia and taking a lock. A function type answers
*undecided* — a lambda is its captures — so this rides on D29's body walk. A
`spawn`ed lambda is handed to our own runtime and runs later, so it is outside
this rule. D32 and D33 are the two halves of one boundary.

### D34 — A type records what reading a value of it touches, in `touches`.

`fs::Mapped` says `touches = ["file read"]`: `mapped[i]` calls nothing a
program wrote, so the fact lives on the type, as `iterates` does
([ADR-025](adr-025.md) D6). It is written by hand and never inferred. **Silence
is not a claim**: a type with no `touches` is not refused on, because what is
built on it is a refusal (Part III C.4).

### D35 — Reading such a value inside a door is `NK2201`.

Both shapes of read, a method and an index. The message names the page fault,
says why `NK2202` (a pause) and `NK2203` (a lock) are silent, and hands over the
way out: read what the block needs before the door. Part II 12.2's *no I/O
while holding locked data* has a code for each of its three cases.

### The runtime check

### D36 — The runtime re-entrancy check is a build option, `reentrancy-check` in `[build]`, `yes` or `no`, `yes` by default.

Under D28 it cannot fire in a correct compiler; it is **self-control of D28,
not error handling**, and without it a compiler hole shows as a silent hang.
For every program that obeys D28 both builds behave identically. It is a
guarantee that can be declined ([ADR-292](adr-292.md) D7), not a debug aid. It
lives in the manifest, with **no command-line override**, because a shipped
build that could be changed from the command line is not reproducible; and in
`[build]` rather than the runtime settings, because it changes what the
compiler emits. A third spelling is refused, not guessed at. Part I 1.2 names
the build options rather than counting them.

### D37 — It is a dimension of the build cache and of the compiled `std`'s own tree.

A switch is a cache-key dimension ([ADR-037](adr-037.md) D4), which adds one to
[ADR-021](adr-021.md) D5's enumeration. And because the check lives in
`nikaia-std`, the word decides what that crate contains: without a tree of its
own, a second project would link the first one's answer
([ADR-021](adr-021.md) D7).

### D38 — Declining it reaches the crossing shape alone.

The mutex shape carries a mark this compiler wrote, and that is what the switch
declines. The cheap shape is a `RefCell`, whose borrow flag notices a
re-entrant borrow whatever anyone asks: the language below's, not this
option's.

## 5. Consequences

* **Part II 12.2's counter and 12.3's transfer are programs**, at both
  settings: a `SharedMut` made by a call, captured by tasks
  ([ADR-045](adr-045.md) D2), changed through `update` and `update_all`.
* **The lost update cannot be written** — in two lines or two hundred, in one
  function or across a request — without the compiler naming it, and nothing
  of it exists at run time. `Overtaken` exists only where a program wrote
  `after:`.
* **A change to locked data is written in `update`, with `mut`.** A program
  that changed a large value in place says `update` and the word; `push` on a
  large value is one line, a lock and the push.
* **The codes:** `NK1123` (D3), `NK1124` (D18), `NK1141` and `NK1138` at a
  door (D12), `NK2201` (D35), `NK2203` (D28-D29), `NK2204` (D20), `NK2205` and
  `NK2207` (D25), `NK2208` (D26), `NK2503` (D32).
* **What it costs:** the seed list of `contracts::sharing` carries twice the
  weight — a missed crossing is now unsoundness for the lock as well as for the
  count (D7); a coarse lock property refuses some programs a precise one would
  accept (D30); `Seen[…]` is written at a field that keeps a read value and at
  a parameter of a lock-touching function (D24).

The numbers that decided, one line each, with the method in the note:

* **11.3 ns** an uncontended acquisition for a mutex floor at `no`, no system
  call, zero `futex` calls in a million acquisitions — what D4 and D6 decline
  ([`mutex-floor.md`](../../mutex-floor.md)).
* **+2.0 ns, ×1.15** for D9's owner check as designed
  ([`mutex-floor.md`](../../mutex-floor.md) §4.3); what the shipped check
  costs is in [`lock-free.md`](../../lock-free.md).
* **×0.63–×0.67** of the mutex for the `word` row uncontended, several times
  less under contention, and **×6.5 worse** than the cheap single-thread shape
  — why D8 never replaces the cheap shape
  ([`lock-free.md`](../../lock-free.md)).
* **Three words** to move a `Vec` into and out of a block, whatever its length
  — why `access` needs no mutation ([ADR-281](adr-281.md)).
* **One of nine** `std` entries whose touch set names I/O neither pauses nor
  takes a lock: reading a `fs::Mapped` (D34, [ADR-281](adr-281.md)).

## 6. What this does not decide

* **A bare `Locked[T]` outside a `Shared`.** It has one owner and takes the
  floor because the analysis has no entry for it — safe and wasteful
  ([ADR-281](adr-281.md)).
* **Whether blocking to acquire a lock counts as pausing.** It does not, and
  that is not this record's: the rule is *no suspension point while a lock is
  held* ([ADR-297](adr-297.md) D10, [ADR-005](adr-005.md) D6).
* **`Locked[Shared[T]]`**, a lock around a handle: a different type, probably
  useless, still permitted.
* **Three locks or more** (try-and-back-off, as C++'s `std::lock`), and **which
  pairs of types a target could swap in one step**.
* **What poisoning offers at `yes`** — recover, reset, or end the process — a
  runtime setting rather than a door; whoever offers *recover* offers a copy
  taken before the block.
* **Readers that never wait, a door that does not wait, a timeout, fairness,
  and waiting for a state** (a condition variable): options after the `;` or
  doors of their own.
* **A witness from the wrong lock** (`a.set(x; after: b_seen)`), **a version
  counted at run time** for a large witness, and **what `Seen` means to a
  foreign crate**, whose reviewed `touches` line ([ADR-290](adr-290.md))
  answers for it.
* **A typed `catch`** that retries `Overtaken` and lets another failure past.
* **A write through a field or an index**, `a.b = 42` with a hull behind `b`:
  `NK2204` asks of a name only.
* **D33's *stored* lambda**, which the `locks` column cannot answer for without
  refusing correct programs, and widens the day the type grammar's function
  types are kept by a callee.
* **A precise *which lock* analysis**, whose home would be `touches` (D30).
* **Whether declining the re-entrancy check is worth anything measured** (D36).
* **A way to accept a page fault inside a door**, and anything about
  `fs::Mapped` outside one (D35).
